What this means

1

The trust gap sits at the top, not the classroom.

Educators and school leadership both score around 4 out of 5. District leadership drops to 3.66, and "enough explanation for district decisions" is the single lowest score in the whole survey (3.19/5). The further a decision is from the classroom, the less understood it feels.

2

Transparency, specifically, is the fix.

It's the weakest of the four trust statements quantitatively (32.7% disagree) and it's the #1 open-text theme across three separate questions. Not "communicate more" — communicate more openly about how and why decisions get made.

3

Email is earning its place. The newsletter isn't, quite.

Email leads on both use and effectiveness — a genuinely well-matched channel. Talk 203 Newsletter is used by over half of respondents but rated effective by less than a third of them. Worth asking what's in it, and whether it's the right format for what people actually want.

4

Budget silence reads as evasion, not disinterest.

Budget/finance ranks lower as a stated priority (38.6%) than safety or policy — but it's one of the most common specific asks in every open-text question. People aren't demanding budget updates by default; they notice sharply when the numbers aren't explained.

5

People want to be asked before, not told after.

"Explain board/district decisions" recurs as a theme independent of transparency generally — a specific ask for input windows before votes, not just better after-the-fact explanations.

6

Community and business stakeholders are the most disconnected group in the survey.

Only 17 responses, but 76% call district communication "not very" or "not at all" effective for their organization — the single worst effectiveness score anywhere in this data. Small sample, but a group worth a deliberate outreach plan rather than the default parent-facing channels.

7

The teacher–parent handoff breaks down at secondary school.

Showed up unprompted in the closing "what are we missing" question: consistency drops noticeably once students move from elementary into middle and high school, right as communication shifts from one teacher to many. Worth a specific secondary-level communication standard.
